The Nature of Calendars

How people ordered time with the sun, the moon and a little mathematics

← Back Part 1 of 3 Continue →

The term “calendarology” can loosely describe the study of calendars: how they arise, why different cultures build them differently, what role they play in religion, politics and everyday life, and how they help people find shared rhythms. Behind every calendar lies not only mathematics but also tradition, power and social order. In this sense a calendar is more than a timekeeper — it is a cultural technique with which people structure their shared future.

Imagine having to promise someone a meeting “in eighty sunrises” — and not being allowed to lose count. That is exactly the problem humanity faced for tens of thousands of years. A calendar is, at heart, an agreed solution: a set of rules that cuts the endless flow of time into manageable portions — days, weeks, months, years.

Importantly, there is no single “the” calendar. Over the millennia, very different cultures devised very different systems — the Egyptian, Babylonian, Jewish, Islamic, Chinese, Indian, Maya calendars and many more. Each answers the same question in its own way. The fact that most of the world today uses the same calendar in everyday life is a very recent development — more on that at the end of Part 2.

Where the word “calendar” comes from

The word takes us straight to ancient Rome. It comes from Latin calendarium, the “account book” of a moneylender. At its root are the Kalendae (also Calendae) — the first day of every Roman month, when interest and debts traditionally fell due. The Kalendae in turn are linked to the verb calare, “to call out” or “to proclaim”: at the beginning of the month it was publicly announced when the new moon days would fall. From “proclaiming the start of the month” came the “account book,” and from that eventually our word calendar.

Three celestial clocks that refuse to agree

The first clock is the Sun. One rotation of the Earth on its axis gives us the day — the basic unit no one disputes. When the sun stands highest, it is noon; that could already be read thousands of years ago with a simple shadow stick.

The second clock is the Moon. It waxes and wanes in a reliable rhythm of about 29½ days — from new moon to new moon. These lunar phases are so striking that almost everywhere in the world they inspired the invention of the month. “Month” and “moon” are not related by chance.

The third clock is the Sun again, but on the large scale: one full orbit of the Earth around the Sun produces the year with its seasons. For farmers it was a matter of survival to know when to sow and when to harvest.

The whole drama of calendar-making can be summed up in one sentence: the year does not last exactly 365 days, and the month does not fit cleanly into the year.

The flaw in the system

Here the trouble begins. A solar year does not last 365 days but about 365.2422 days — almost a quarter of a day longer. And twelve lunar months add up to only about 354 days, eleven days too few for a full year. The three celestial clocks inevitably drift apart. Anyone who does nothing will see their calendar slowly wander through the seasons.

Every culture had to decide which clock to favour — and that is precisely the reason for the variety of calendars. Pure lunar calendars (like the Islamic one) follow the Moon faithfully and let their festivals drift through the seasons. Solar calendars (like the one in common use today) follow the year and let the months keep only a rough connection to the Moon. Lunisolar calendars (like the Jewish or Chinese ones) try to bridge the gap and occasionally insert a whole intercalary month to reconcile both clocks.

The elegant trick: the leap day

A widespread solution to the awkward quarter-day is elegantly simple. Instead of being a few hours off every year, one collects four quarter-days and adds them as a whole day: the 29th of February. That is why almost every fourth year has an extra day.

The leap-year rule in three lines: A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4 — except when it is divisible by 100, unless it is also divisible by 400. That is why 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not. This fine-tuning is so accurate that it is only one day off after more than 3,000 years.

And where does the week come from?

The week is the odd one out: it has no astronomical cause. Seven days cannot be derived from either the Moon or the Sun. It most likely comes from Babylon, where people revered the seven “wandering stars” visible to the naked eye — the Sun, the Moon and the five classical planets. Their names still live on in our days of the week. The week is therefore the purely human chapter in a story otherwise dictated by the heavens.

In the end a calendar is a small masterpiece of compromise: an age-old peace treaty between the Sun, the Moon and the human desire for order.
