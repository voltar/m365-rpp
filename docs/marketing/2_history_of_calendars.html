A Short History of Calendars

From notches in bone to a worldwide date

Calendars are probably older than writing. Long before people wrote down words, they were already carving time into bone. Stone Age objects carry rows of notches that many researchers interpret as counts of lunar phases — the first attempt to translate the sky into bookkeeping.

Egypt: The star that announced the flood

The ancient Egyptians were masters of sky-watching. Their lives depended on the Nile, which overflowed its banks every year. They noticed that this flood began at almost exactly the moment when the bright star Sirius, after weeks of invisibility, first reappeared in the morning sky. From this observation came one of the first practical solar calendars of 365 days: twelve months of 30 days each, plus five feast days at the end.

Rome: A calendar in chaos

The Romans, by contrast, thoroughly scrambled the reckoning of time. Their old lunar calendar was so inaccurate that priests inserted extra days at will — often for pure political reasons, to lengthen or shorten terms of office. By the middle of the first century BCE the calendar was a full three months out of step with the seasons.

Julius Caesar clears things up

It took a dictator to restore order. Julius Caesar sought Egyptian advice and ordered a radical reform in 46 BCE. To wipe out the accumulated error, that single year had to last a remarkable 445 days — the “Year of Confusion.” After that the Julian calendar applied: 365 days, with a leap day every four years. In his honour the month of July still bears Caesar’s name.

Caesar’s calendar was so good that it remained in use for more than 1,600 years. But “almost right” is, over the centuries, still not right enough.

The Middle Ages turn the calendar into art

In the Middle Ages the calendar became a work of art. Splendid books of hours showed, for each month, the matching rural labour — sowing, harvesting, slaughtering pigs — framed by the signs of the zodiac.

1582: Ten days disappear

The small calculation error in the Julian calendar — roughly eleven minutes of excess length per year — had added up to a full ten days by the 16th century. Pope Gregory XIII drew the line in 1582: Thursday 4 October was followed directly by Friday 15 October. Ten days were simply struck out. At the same time he introduced the refined leap-year rule that still applies today.

How the world arrived at a shared date

And here comes the crucial point: this Gregorian calendar did not become the world standard overnight. Catholic countries followed quickly; Protestant and Orthodox ones hesitated for centuries in some cases — some regions switched only in the 20th century. That is why the Russian “October Revolution” took place in November according to today’s calendar.

The fact that we use the same date worldwide today is a child of the modern age. Only when trade, seafaring, railways, the telegraph and finally the internet drew the world more tightly together did a shared beat of time become a practical necessity. International treaties, timetables, stock exchanges and global communication need a uniform “when.” So the Gregorian calendar established itself as the global administrative and business standard — not because it is the only “correct” one, but because the networked world had to agree on one common denominator.

The other calendars have not disappeared because of this. The Islamic, Jewish, Chinese, Hindu, Buddhist, Persian or Ethiopian calendars still determine festivals, holidays and religious life for billions of people — often running parallel to the Gregorian everyday date. The world thus shares a common business date while continuing to cultivate its cultural diversity of time. A rare and rather beautiful compromise.
